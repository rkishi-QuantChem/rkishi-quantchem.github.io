Learning, Questioning, and Growing Together

A research group is a place where we share questions, learn from one another through dialogue, and create new knowledge together.

At RKQC, we value developing not only the knowledge and technical skills needed for research, but also the ability to think independently, engage with others, and explore unfamiliar problems.

Education — Learning, Questioning, and Growing Together

A research group is not only a place where new knowledge is created. It is also a place where people develop the ability to contribute to the creation of knowledge in the future.

We value an approach to education in which students and their research supervisors within RKQC explore scientific questions together, think from their respective perspectives, and deepen their understanding through dialogue.

Certain parts of theoretical and computational chemistry research can be pursued individually—for example, reflecting on a question, exploring a model, or carrying out a calculation. Yet research does not end when a calculation produces a result. What matters is how we interpret that result, connect it with existing knowledge, and use it to open new questions.

This is why we value dialogue. Dialogue is more than frequent conversation. It can involve articulating an idea, engaging with another person's reasoning, examining a research result, or reconsidering one's understanding in response to a different interpretation. Computational results, figures, models, and papers can all provide a basis for such exchanges.

Within our research group, students and their supervisors bring different questions and perspectives into contact with one another. One person's discovery may lead another person to ask a new question, allowing the understanding of both individuals and the group as a whole to develop. We see this shared development as an essential part of growing together.

People differ in how they think and engage with others. Some enjoy spontaneous discussion, while others need time to reflect and express their ideas carefully. Rather than imposing one way of participating, we value helping each person find forms of dialogue that work for them.

What We Learn Through Research

What we hope students gain from research extends beyond specialized knowledge and computational techniques.

The ability to use quantum chemistry, computational science, programming, and AI provides an important foundation for conducting research and contributing in professional settings. At the same time, technologies and social environments continue to change. Alongside learning the tools that are useful today, we consider it important to develop the ability to approach new challenges and acquire the knowledge and methods they require.

We therefore value the experience of identifying questions, examining the essential aspects of a problem, choosing appropriate methods, and critically evaluating results. Equally important is the ability to engage with natural phenomena, other people's ideas, and information obtained through AI, and to reconsider and develop one's own understanding.

Learning in a research group is not simply about becoming able to solve problems independently. There is also value in sharing ideas in ways suited to each person, learning from different perspectives, and creating new knowledge together.

We hope students develop both practical abilities that enable them to contribute in new environments after graduation and the capacity to continue learning, thinking, and growing over the longer term.

Learning Through Theoretical and Computational Chemistry

To understand molecular structures and properties, intermolecular interactions, and the time evolution of electronic states, we study and apply quantum theory, chemical concepts, mathematical models, and computational methods.

Research provides opportunities to develop abilities such as:

  • Identifying questions: Recognizing unresolved problems in experimental observations, computational results, and existing theories.
  • Developing models: Determining which factors and degrees of freedom are important for understanding a complex phenomenon.
  • Computing and validating: Selecting appropriate methods and examining results with attention to approximations, accuracy, and limitations.
  • Interpreting chemistry: Connecting computational findings with concepts such as electronic structure, chemical bonding, and molecular properties.
  • Sharing ideas: Using results, figures, models, and written explanations to discuss and deepen understanding.
  • Bringing research together: Reflecting on trial and error and presenting what has been learned, including questions that remain unresolved.

Learning to use computational software effectively matters, but it is not an end in itself. We aim to help students develop the ability to think independently, learn what is needed, and combine different approaches when facing unfamiliar problems.

Learning Through Challenges and Setbacks

Research often involves unexpected results and approaches that do not work as anticipated. Such outcomes do not, by themselves, diminish the value of the research.

What matters is examining why an approach did not work, what was learned, and how our understanding should change. When the process is accurately recorded, critically examined, and clearly explained, even an unsuccessful attempt can contribute knowledge that informs future work.

Undergraduate and graduate research must meet the academic standards of the relevant degree programs. However, we do not believe that the educational value of research should be judged solely by whether it produces the desired results.

How, then, should we decide which challenges are worth pursuing?

We consider it important to imagine what new understanding or exciting possibilities a line of research might open. Even if its immediate objectives cannot be achieved, it may become the starting point for new questions and future investigations.

Of course, an intriguing idea is not sufficient reason to pursue every proposed project. We must also consider current knowledge and methods, the time and resources available, research risks, and where a meaningful investigation might begin.

Students should not be left to make these judgments alone. Research supervisors can propose possible research directions and future developments, while students contribute their own ideas and perspectives. A student's insight may also reveal possibilities beyond what a supervisor originally imagined. Envisioning an interesting future for a research project and exploring how to move toward it are therefore shared activities.

We value supporting students as they explore unfamiliar problems, learn from setbacks, and bring their research into a coherent form that can contribute to future questions and investigations.

When research becomes difficult, appropriate support matters. Research supervisors in RKQC have a responsibility to provide a supportive research environment while respecting students' independence and their capacity to grow.

We also hope that, through their experiences in the research group, students will continue to identify new questions, engage with others, and contribute to the creation of knowledge wherever their paths lead.
